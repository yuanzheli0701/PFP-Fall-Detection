[P27] (Heading 1) Introduction and problem statement
[P28] (Normal) Falls are a major health risk for older adults. According to figures reported by the World Health Organization and cited by Taramasco et al. (2025), about 28–35% of people over 65 fall each year, rising to 32–42% after 70. The consequences range from fractures to long periods on the floor, and many older people develop a lasting fear of falling. Automatic fall detection systems (FDS) aim to raise an alarm quickly, especially for people who live alone.
[P29] (Normal) Most recent FDS rely on machine learning, but they face a common data problem. Everyday activities such as walking or sitting are easy to record in large quantities. Falls, in contrast, are rare, hard to capture in real conditions and costly to simulate realistically. Public fall datasets are therefore small and strongly imbalanced, and they usually involve only a few subjects. A classical supervised classifier trained on such data tends to favor the majority class and generalizes poorly to new people.
[P30] (Normal) Metric learning offers an alternative. Instead of learning a direct mapping from a signal to a class label, it learns a representation space in which similar events are close and different events are far apart. Classification is then done by comparing distances, for instance to the nearest neighbors or to class prototypes. This family of methods was designed for settings with few examples per class, which makes it a promising candidate for fall detection.
[P31] (Normal) This review supports a project that compares these two approaches on the QUIDA multimodal dataset (Taramasco et al., 2025), using two sensors: a body-worn accelerometer and a millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar. It has four objectives:
[P32] (Normal) 1.	Present the two sensing modalities and the QUIDA dataset.
[P33] (Normal) 2.	Review supervised deep learning baselines for fall detection (CNN and LSTM).
[P34] (Normal) 3.	Review metric learning methods: siamese networks, contrastive and triplet losses, and classification by nearest neighbors or prototypes.
[P35] (Normal) 4.	Review multimodal fusion strategies, then justify the methods selected for the project.
[P37] (Heading 1) Sensing modalities for fall detection
[P38] (Normal) Fall detection sensors are usually grouped into three families: wearable, ambient and visual (Taramasco et al., 2025). Visual sensors such as RGB cameras are effective but raise privacy concerns. This project uses one wearable sensor and one ambient sensor, both of which preserve privacy.
[P40] (Heading 2) Wearable sensors: accelerometers
[P41] (Normal) Tri-axial accelerometers are the most established modality for fall detection. A fall produces a characteristic signature: a short free-fall phase, a sharp impact peak, then a period of inactivity. They are cheap, embedded in every smartphone, and give a dense, high-frequency signal. Their main limitation is practical: the device must be worn and charged, otherwise monitoring stops. Their placement also matters; a sensor near the body's center of mass, such as on the trunk or waist, gives the most representative signal.
[P43] (Heading 2) Ambient sensors: mmWave radar
[P44] (Normal) Millimeter-wave radars operating around 60 GHz detect people without contact and without producing identifiable images. They work in the dark, and the user has nothing to wear. Modern radars output a sparse 3D point cloud in which each point carries a position and a Doppler velocity. The Doppler component directly measures the speed of moving body parts, which makes the sudden high-velocity motion of a fall stand out. Jin et al. (2020) showed that a 4D mmWave point cloud, processed as a sequence of frames, supports reliable fall detection.
[P45] (Normal) Radar data are harder to process than accelerometer data. The number of detected points changes from one frame to the next, so the raw data do not have a fixed size. Two main strategies exist: aggregating each frame into fixed hand-crafted features (point count, mean Doppler, centroid height), or using networks designed for point sets such as PointNet (Qi et al., 2017). The second option needs much more training data.

[P48] (Normal) Several public datasets combine multiple sensors, but very few include privacy-preserving ambient sensors. Table 1 compares QUIDA with the multimodal datasets reviewed by Taramasco et al. (2025).

[P54] (Normal) QUIDA was recorded with ten young participants (8 men, 2 women, aged about 25) who each simulated ten types of falls: backward, forward and lateral, caused by tripping, fainting or sitting down, with straight or bent knees.  The accelerometer is a smartphone attached to the trunk between the T5 and T10 vertebrae and sampled at 62.5 Hz. The radar (Texas Instruments IWR6843AOP, 60–64 GHz) is mounted on the ceiling and runs at 8.3 Hz. Each radar point is described by its position (x, y, z), Doppler velocity, signal-to-noise ratio, noise level and tracking ID. Sensors were synchronized using a shared arm-flapping movement that creates a simultaneous peak in all signals.
[P55] (Normal) They do not train any classifier. They only characterize the signals, and find that the instantaneous signal norm separates falls from non-falls best for the radar and the accelerometer, because both sensors measure motion directly (velocity and acceleration). This supports the choice of these two sensors for the present project.
[P56] (Normal) QUIDA also has clear limitations: only ten young and healthy subjects, simulated falls that are more repetitive than real ones, class imbalance, and no published classification benchmark. The project uses an augmented version of the dataset provided by the supervisors, which partly compensates for the small size.
[P57] (Heading 1) Supervised deep learning for fall detection
[P58] (Normal) Early fall detectors used thresholds on the acceleration magnitude or hand-crafted features fed to classical classifiers (SVM, random forests). Deep learning has since become the standard, because it learns features directly from raw or lightly processed signals. Two architectures dominate for sensor time series: convolutional neural networks (CNN) and long short-term memory networks (LSTM) (Wang et al., 2019).
[P60] (Heading 2) CNN-based approaches
[P61] (Normal) A one-dimensional CNN slides learned filters along the time axis of a signal window. Each filter detects a local pattern, such as the sharp impact peak of a fall, wherever it appears in the window. Stacking layers combines these local patterns into higher-level features. For accelerometer-based fall detection, CNNs have reached high accuracy on public datasets while keeping a small number of parameters (Santos et al., 2019). They are fast to train, easy to run on embedded devices, and less prone to overfitting than larger models, which matters when data are scarce.
[P64] (Heading 2) LSTM-based approaches
[P65] (Normal) Recurrent neural networks process a sequence step by step and keep an internal memory of what came before. The LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) adds gates that control what the memory keeps or forgets, which solves the vanishing gradient problem of simple recurrent networks. LSTMs are well suited to data where the order of events matters. A fall is a sequence of phases: normal motion, rapid descent, impact, then lying still. Musci et al. (2021) used recurrent networks for online fall detection on wearable devices. For radar, Jin et al. (2020) combined a recurrent autoencoder with a sequence of point-cloud frames. Hybrid CNN-LSTM models also exist, where the CNN extracts local features and the LSTM models their evolution over time.
[P68] (Heading 2) Limitations with scarce and imbalanced data
[P69] (Normal) These supervised models share three weaknesses in the fall detection setting:
[P70] (Normal) •	Class imbalance. With three non-falls for each fall in QUIDA, and far more in real life, a model can reach high accuracy while missing many falls. Common fixes include class weights, oversampling and data augmentation, but they do not change the underlying problem.
[P71] (Normal) •	Few subjects. With only ten participants, the model risks learning the movement style of specific people rather than the general signature of a fall. Performance on unseen subjects, measured with leave-one-subject-out validation, is often much lower than on random splits.
[P72] (Normal) •	Fixed classes. A classifier's output layer is tied to the classes seen during training. Adding a new type of fall or adapting to a new person requires retraining.
[P73] (Normal) These limitations motivate metric learning, which is designed to learn from few examples and to classify by comparison rather than by a fixed output layer.
[P74] (Heading 1) Metric learning
[P75] (Heading 2) Principle
[P76] (Normal) Metric learning trains a neural network f to map each input x to an embedding vector f(x), usually of low dimension and normalized to unit length. The network is trained so that the distance between two embeddings reflects how similar the inputs are: two falls should be close, a fall and a walking window should be far apart. The network is not asked to output a class directly. Classification happens afterwards, by comparing distances in the embedding space.
[P77] (Normal) This changes how the data is used. A supervised classifier learns from individual labelled examples. A metric learning model learns from pairs or triplets of examples. With N examples, the number of possible pairs grows as N², so a small dataset still provides many training signals. This is why metric learning is widely used in few-shot learning (Koch et al., 2015; Snell et al., 2017).
[P79] (Heading 2) Siamese networks and contrastive loss
[P80] (Normal) A siamese network is made of two identical branches that share the same weights (Chicco, 2021). Each branch receives one input of a pair and produces its embedding. Since the weights are shared, both inputs are projected into the same space, and the distance d between the two embeddings is computed. The contrastive loss (Hadsell et al., 2006) trains the network on labelled pairs:
[P81] (MATH) text='' || L=y*d2+1-y*max⁡(0,m-d)²
[P82] (Normal) Here y = 1 if the two inputs belong to the same class and y = 0 otherwise, and m is a margin. Similar pairs are pulled together, while dissimilar pairs are pushed apart until their distance exceeds m. Pairs that are already far enough apart no longer contribute to the loss. Koch et al. (2015) showed that siamese networks can recognise new classes from a single example, which established them as a reference for few-shot problems.
[P84] (Heading 2) Triplet networks and triplet loss
[P85] (Normal) The triplet loss, popularised by FaceNet (Schroff et al., 2015), works on three examples at once: an anchor a, a positive p of the same class, and a negative n of a different class. It requires the anchor to be closer to the positive than to the negative, by at least a margin α:
[P86] (MATH) text='' || L=max⁡(0, fa-fp2-fa-fn2+α)
[P87] (Normal) The contrastive loss constrains absolute distances, whereas the triplet loss only constrains relative ones. This gives the embedding space more freedom and often leads to better representations. The main difficulty is the choice of triplets. Most random triplets are too easy: they already satisfy the margin and produce no gradient. FaceNet therefore selects "semi-hard" negatives, which are farther than the positive but still inside the margin. Hermans et al. (2017) proposed "batch-hard" mining, which takes the hardest positive and negative within each batch. They also recommend building batches with P classes and K examples per class, which guarantees that every class is represented in each batch. This balanced sampling is especially relevant for imbalanced problems like fall detection.
[P89] (Heading 2) Classification in embedding space
[P90] (Normal) Once the embedding network is trained, a new input is classified by comparing its embedding to those labelled reference examples. Two strategies are common.
[P91] (Heading 3) Nearest-neighbor classification (k-NN)
[P92] (Normal) The k-NN classifier finds the k reference embeddings closest to the query and assigns the majority class among them. It needs no extra training and naturally handles complex class shapes. However, it is sensitive to noisy examples and to class imbalance: when non-falls outnumber falls, the nearest neighbours of an ambiguous window are more likely to be non-falls. Its cost also grows with the number of stored references.
[P93] (Heading 3) Prototype-based classification
[P94] (Normal) Prototypical networks (Snell et al., 2017) represent each class k by a single prototype ck, the mean of the embeddings of its reference examples Sk:
[P95] (MATH) text='' || ck= 1|Sk|xi∈Sknf(xi)⁡
[P96] (Normal) A query is assigned to the class whose prototype is closest. Each class has exactly one representative, whatever its number of examples, so the decision is not biased towards the majority class. Prototypes are also cheap to compute and easy to update adding a new person's falls simply shifts the fall prototype. The limitation is that a single mean can poorly represent a class with several distinct modes, for example forward and lateral falls. Using several prototypes per class, one per fall type, is a possible extension.
[P100] (Heading 2) Few-shot fall detection
[P101] (Normal) A few studies have already applied metric learning to fall detection. Droghini et al. (2018) trained a siamese network on audio features and showed that falls can be detected with very few fall examples available for training. Bakshi and Rajan (2021) proposed a shallow siamese network for few-shot fall detection, showing that a compact model can learn a useful similarity function from a limited number of fall samples. Both works confirm that the few-shot framing suits fall detection. To our knowledge, metric learning has not yet been applied to the QUIDA dataset, nor to the fusion of wearable and radar data for fall detection.
[P102] (Heading 1) Multimodal sensor fusion
[P103] (Normal) Combining sensors of different natures can reduce both false alarms and missed falls, since each sensor compensates for the blind spots of the others (Wang et al., 2020; Taramasco et al., 2025). An accelerometer captures the body's own motion but fails if it is not worn. A radar watches the room but can be disturbed by other moving objects or by the person leaving its field of view. Fusion strategies are usually classified by the level at which the sensors are combined.
[P104] (List Paragraph) Early fusion (data level). Raw or preprocessed signals are merged into a single input before any model. It is simple, but it requires all signals to share the same time base and format.
[P105] (List Paragraph) Intermediate fusion (feature level). Each sensor has its own encoder, and the learned features or embeddings are concatenated before a shared final layer. Each encoder can be adapted to its data type.
[P106] (List Paragraph) Late fusion (decision level). Each sensor has its own complete model, and their output (probabilities, scores or distances) are combined, for example by averaging or voting. It is robust to a missing sensor, but it cannot learn interactions between modalities.
[P107] (Normal) The main technical obstacle in QUIDA is the difference in sampling rates: 62.5 Hz for the accelerometer versus 8.3 Hz for the radar, a ratio of about 7.5. Early fusion would require either downsampling the accelerometer, losing the fine shape of the impact peak, or interpolating the radar, creating artificial values. Intermediate and late fusion avoid this problem, because each branch processes its sensor at its native rate over the same time window. QUIDA's segmentation already provides synchronised windows of equal duration for all sensors, with a different number of samples per sensor.
[P108] (Normal) Intermediate fusion fits naturally with metric learning. The two sensor encoders can be trained jointly so that the concatenated embedding satisfies the contrastive or triplet loss. The fused embedding space can then be used with the same k-NN or prototype classifiers as in the single-sensor case.
[P110] (Heading 1) Synthesis and methodological choices
[P111] (Normal) The project will compare a supervised baseline and a metric learning model that share the same encoder architecture, first for each sensor separately and then fused.
[P115] (Heading 2) Selected Methods
[P116] (Normal) Supervised baselines. For the accelerometer, a small 1D-CNN applied to 4 s windows (about 250 samples × 3 axes). It captures the impact peak with few parameters. For the radar, each frame will first be summarised by a fixed set of features: number of points, mean and maximum Doppler velocity, mean height, centroid position and point spread. The resulting sequence (about 33 frames × 8 features) will feed a small LSTM, which models the temporal evolution of the fall (drop in height, Doppler peak). Point-set networks such as PointNet are not retained, as they need more data than QUIDA provides. If time allows, an LSTM will also be tested on the accelerometer to compare the two architectures on the same sensor.
[P117] (Normal) Metric learning models. The embedding network of each sensor will reuse the corresponding baseline encoder, without its classification layer. The only difference between the two approaches will therefore be the training objective and the decision rule. The main loss will be the triplet loss with balanced P×K batches and semi-hard negative mining, since balanced sampling directly addresses the 1:3 imbalance. A siamese network with contrastive loss will be implemented first as a simpler reference.
[P118] (Normal) Classification. Prototype-based classification will be the main decision rule, because it is robust to imbalance. k-NN will be evaluated on the same embeddings for comparison. Both need no extra training.
[P119] (Normal) Fusion. Intermediate fusion by concatenating the accelerometer and radar embeddings, trained jointly with the triplet loss. Each branch keeps its native sampling rate, so no resampling is needed. Late fusion (averaging the scores of the two single-sensor models) will serve as a simple fusion baseline.
[P120] (Heading 2) Evaluation protocol
[P121] (List Paragraph) Leave-one-subject-out cross-validation: ten folds, each testing on a subject never seen in training.
[P122] (List Paragraph) Identical partitions for all methods, so that differences come only from the models.
[P124] (List Paragraph) Metrics: recall on falls (sensitivity), precision, F1-score and specificity. Accuracy alone is misleading with imbalanced classes.
[P126] (List Paragraph) No leakage from augmentation: augmented copies of the test subject's recordings must never appear in the training set.
